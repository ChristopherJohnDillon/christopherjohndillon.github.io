Between roughly the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries, in parts of Wales, the English border counties, and Scotland, a destitute man might be summoned to a funeral and asked to perform a peculiar service. A piece of bread (and, depending on the parish, ale, milk, or in some cases a baked potato) would be placed on or near the corpse. The bread was believed to absorb the sins of the deceased. The sin-eater would eat it, take the sins onto himself, murmur something to the effect of "I give easement and rest now to thee, dear man", and accept the four or six pence he was paid for the trouble. He would then very often be driven from the house with sticks, never to be invited back except for the next funeral. The earliest written record we have is from John Aubrey in 1686; the last documented sin-eater was Richard Munslow, a Shropshire farmer who died in 1906 and whose grave at Ratlinghope was restored, with some ceremony, in 2010.

What the sin-eater offered was a transactional, externalised absolution. The family did not have to grieve any unconfessed sins their loved one might still be carrying. The community did not have to look the dirty work in the face. A man with no other prospects took on the burden, was paid a pittance, and was then shunned. Everyone got what they wanted, except — fairly obviously — the sin-eater.

I have a working theory that data governance, done badly, is the same transaction.

Every organisation produces dirty data. Categories are inconsistent or missing entirely, mandatory fields go unfilled, the same SKU has three different descriptions across three systems, discounts are recorded against orphan line items rather than against the products they apply to, customers are tagged inconsistently or not at all. At some point the reporting layer begins to produce numbers that don't reconcile, the dashboards quietly start contradicting each other, and the question arises: whose mess is this?

The temptation, at this point, is to find a sin-eater. Appoint someone — often a junior, frequently someone with no domain knowledge, almost always someone who can't refuse — and call them the Data Owner. Hand them a spreadsheet of issues. Tell them they are now accountable. The rest of the business carries on absolved, the data sins now belong to one person sat in the corner with the bread on their lap, and you can put their name on the appropriate slide whenever the question of "do we have data governance" is raised. (Yes. Look. We have a Data Owner.)

This breaks down, for a reason that is no great mystery. A sin-eater could absorb sins because the transaction was symbolic; nobody actually believed the bread literally cleansed anything, they believed it transferred a metaphysical burden. Data, by contrast, is real. A Data Owner without domain knowledge cannot fix a category that is wrong, because they have no way of knowing what the right category looks like. A Data Owner without the authority to refuse a non-compliant item being entered into NetSuite has no way to maintain the standard. A Data Owner on whom the role has been bolted as an unpaid extra to an already full job will skip the quarterly audit, because nobody has space for an audit on top of their actual work. The role becomes ceremonial, the data stays dirty, the reports continue to contradict each other. The only thing that has changed is that there is now a name to point at when the next reconciliation fails — which is, on reflection, the part of the arrangement that the village wanted from the sin-eater in the first place.

What good data governance actually looks like is, to my mind, much less mysterious than the volume of consultancy spent on it would suggest.

You have to start by educating people about why data hygiene matters in the first place. People only maintain data they can see the point of, and "because the data team has asked us to" falls some way short of being a reason. A buyer keying in items will fill in mandatory fields if those fields obviously feed into the out-of-stock report they look at every Monday morning. They will ignore them entirely if the fields read like bureaucratic noise. The conversation has to start with what we are trying to learn about the business, what data we need to learn it, and how each person's work contributes to that. Done this way it looks less like a punishment dressed up as a process and more like showing colleagues their contribution to something they themselves benefit from, which is rather more dignifying than handing them a spreadsheet and asking them to please be the bad guy.

The matter of who actually owns the data is where most organisations come unstuck. The Data Owner needs to be someone with domain knowledge — they have to know what good looks like for the data they own and where the edge cases are. They need authority, of the kind that means they can refuse a non-compliant item or customer being entered into the source system and have the refusal stick. They need capacity, which is to say the role is scoped into their actual job rather than tacked on as an unpaid extra. Where it makes sense, the accountable Data Owner (senior, sets the standard, approves changes) is paired with a Data Steward (operational, executes day to day). This pairing is well-established practice in DAMA-DMBOK and elsewhere, and it exists in part precisely because the alternative is the trap I have just described.

The bit most often forgotten is what the Data Owner gets back. When the data is maintained properly, the people maintaining it get something out of the arrangement. Buyers see where they are losing margin. Account managers see which customer segments behave differently and which are worth more of their time. Operations sees where stockouts are actually costing money, with hard numbers in place of intuition. Insights stop being something the data team hands down from above and start being something the business gets out of its own discipline. The Data Owner is no longer carrying a burden so the rest of the business can look away; they are maintaining the inputs that produce the outputs that make their own work easier and better informed.

The Welsh sin-eater walked away with sixpence and a soul full of other people's misdeeds, and was hated for it. I would rather not be in the business of finding sin-eaters. The version worth building is the other one — where the person who owns the data has the knowledge and authority to look after it, and gets a clearer view of their own work in return for the trouble.
